Abstract

As the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) continues to evolve, multi-access edge computing (MEC) brings computation devices closer to end users by deploying them at edge computation devices. This proximity enables faster and more reliable computing services. In MEC-enabled IoV networks, task offloading is crucial to reduce the computational load on vehicles and has attracted increasing attention from researchers. However, existing methods typically assume that all computation devices can transmit execution results to each other. In this work, we address a more realistic challenge of offloading subtasks with hop constraints. We divide each task into multiple subtasks with a tree-structured dependency graph. We model the network as a communication graph in which direct transmission between computation devices is only possible when an edge exists, without assuming full connectivity. We then propose a method that maps the dependency graph to the communication graph, and the goal is to minimize the makespan, i.e., the completion time of the last finishing subtask. Experimental results demonstrate that our method achieves a 17.8% average makespan reduction over existing methods across varying system parameters.

The Problem: Full Connectivity Is Not a Given

Modern vehicular applications such as driving assistance, autonomous driving, HD mapping and entertainment consist of many cooperating subtasks. Offloading these subtasks to different computation devices increases parallelism, but each subtask can only start once it has received the results of all its predecessors. The common schedule-then-offload paradigm fixes a topological-sort-based priority first and then picks a device for each subtask.

These methods implicitly assume that every pair of computation devices can exchange results. In real IoV networks, limited bandwidth and unstable links mean devices are only partially connected, and data may traverse at most \(H\) hops. Under this constraint, a topological-sort order can paint itself into a corner.

Why priority order matters. Given a communication graph and a subtask graph, (a) a topological-sort-based priority may offload the green and blue subtasks first; if they land on devices that share no common neighbor, the pink subtask cannot receive both results, no matter where it is placed. (b) The proposed BFS-based priority guarantees that when a subtask is offloaded, at most one adjacent subtask has already been placed, so a feasible device always exists.

Formally, we build a raw graph \(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{raw}} = (C, L^{\mathrm{raw}})\) from direct V2V, V2M and M2M links, and derive the equivalent single-hop communication graph by connecting every pair of devices that can reach each other within \(H\) hops:

\[ L = \bigl\{ (c, c') \mid c, c' \in C,\; d_{\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{raw}}}(c, c') \le H \bigr\}, \qquad \mathcal{G} = (C, L). \]

System Model

Computation devices \(C = V \cup M\) include vehicles \(V\) (CPU frequency \(F_v\)) and MEC servers \(M\) deployed at roadside units (CPU frequency \(F_m\)). The ego vehicle generates a task whose subtasks form a tree \(G = (N, E)\); subtask \(n\) needs \(W_n\) CPU cycles, and edge \(e_{n,n'}\) carries \(S_{n,n'}\) bytes of intermediate results.

  • Heterogeneous links. V2V and V2M rates follow Shannon capacity with distance-based path loss; M2M links use a fixed fiber rate \(R_{m,m'}\).
  • Computation queueing. A device executes subtasks non-preemptively, so each subtask waits for the earliest available time \(\tau_c\) of its device.
  • Hop-limited communication. Results can only be sent between devices adjacent in \(\mathcal{G}\).

If subtask \(n\) is offloaded to device \(c\) (\(y_{n,c}=1\)), its start and completion times are

\[ t^{\#}_{n,c} = \max\!\Bigl( \tau_c,\; \max_{n' \in \mathrm{Pred}(n),\, y_{n',c'}=1} \Bigl( t^{*}_{n',c'} + \frac{S_{n,n'}}{R_{c,c'}} \Bigr) \Bigr), \qquad t^{*}_{n,c} = t^{\#}_{n,c} + \frac{W_n}{F_c}, \]

and the goal is to find the offloading decision \(Y\) that minimizes the makespan:

\[ \min_{Y}\; \max_{n \in N}\; t^{*}_{n}. \]

Method: BFS-Based Dependent Task Offloading

Our centralized method runs on the ego vehicle, which has a global view of the subtask graph \(G\) and the communication graph \(\mathcal{G}\). It decouples when a subtask runs from in which order it is offloaded, in three phases.

Phase 1

Execution priority

Traverse the reversed tree from sinks to roots and assign each subtask its critical-path length to the end.

Phase 2

BFS offloading priority

Offload subtasks in BFS order over \(G\), so each new subtask touches at most one already-placed neighbor.

Phase 3

Greedy offloading

Try every reachable device, re-simulate the schedule with the reassignment procedure, and keep the one with the lowest makespan.

Phase 1 — Execution Priority

Following an upward-rank formulation, each subtask's priority accounts for its average computation time and the average transmission time toward its successors:

\[ P(n) = \max_{n' \in \mathrm{Succ}(n)} \Bigl( P(n') + \Delta_n + \frac{S_{n,n'}}{R_{n,n'}} \Bigr), \qquad \Delta_n = \frac{1}{|C|} \sum_{c \in C} \frac{W_n}{F_c}. \]

Building the reversed graph \(G^{T}\) and visiting nodes from sinks to roots guarantees that all successors are scored before their parent, in \(O(|N| + |E|)\) time.

Phase 2 — BFS Offloading Priority Guarantees Feasibility

Given a partial assignment \(A \subseteq N\), let \(\mathcal{B}(n) = \{ n' \in A \mid (n,n') \in E \text{ or } (n',n) \in E \}\) be the already-assigned neighbors of subtask \(n\).

Theorem 1. If \(|\mathcal{B}(n)| \le 1\) when assigning subtask \(n\), a feasible device assignment for \(n\) always exists.
If \(\mathcal{B}(n)\) is empty, \(n\) can be placed on any device connected to the ego vehicle. Otherwise, \(n\) only needs a device adjacent in \(\mathcal{G}\) to the device hosting its single assigned neighbor, and such a device always exists.
Theorem 2. When subtasks are processed in BFS order on \(G\), \(|\mathcal{B}(n)| \le 1\) holds for every subtask \(n\).
If \(n\) had two assigned neighbors \(u, v\), BFS would already have connected them through a path not passing through \(n\); together with edges \((u,n)\) and \((n,v)\) this forms a cycle, contradicting that \(G\) is a tree.

Hence the BFS order guarantees a feasible schedule regardless of the network topology. The BFS starts from the subtask with the highest execution priority and expands neighbors through a priority queue keyed by \(P(n)\).

Phase 3 — Greedy Offloading with Reassignment

The first subtask may be offloaded to any neighbor of the ego vehicle; every later subtask is restricted to neighbors of the device hosting the subtask that discovered it in BFS. For each candidate device, we compute the resulting makespan and choose the minimum.

Because the BFS offloading order differs from the actual execution order, start times cannot be accumulated incrementally. A reassignment procedure therefore re-simulates all offloaded subtasks in execution-priority order, including data dependencies and device queueing, to obtain the exact makespan of each candidate. The overall complexity is \(O\bigl(|N|\,|C|\,(|N| + |E|)\bigr)\).

Key Observations

  • Sparser networks hurt everyone, but us least. As the area grows from 500 m to 2000 m, fewer device pairs stay connected and makespan rises for all methods; ours stays lowest throughout.
  • Our method scales with resources. The gain over PBTSA grows with more vehicles (12.3% → 17.3%) and more MEC servers (up to 19.2%), showing that we exploit additional devices better.
  • Robust to the hop limit. Our makespan stays around 0.52 s for every \(H\), while PBTSA depends strongly on \(H\); the gains are largest when connectivity is tightest (\(H = 1\): 22.4%).
  • Informed placement matters. A larger candidate set helps the greedy strategy but hurts Random, whose makespan rises with \(|V|\), \(|M|\) and \(H\). Device selection, not just feasibility, drives performance.
